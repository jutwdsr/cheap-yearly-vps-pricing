## How much does a cheap yearly VPS actually cost?

Two markets share the phrase "cheap yearly VPS," and mixing them up is the most common way people overpay or end up with something unusable.

The first market is the true bottom: NAT VPS plans at $7 to $15 a year. KhanWebHost's $6.99/year plan gives you 1 vCPU, 256MB of RAM, 5GB of SSD and 20 usable ports on a shared IPv4. CloudServer's $10/year plan gives 1 vCPU, 1GB of RAM and 20GB of SSD. These are real products, and for a hobby box or a throwaway sandbox they're fine. What they usually don't give you is a dedicated IPv4 address, which rules out a lot once you start pointing a domain at the machine.

The second market, where 666Clouds sits, is $80 to $270 a year. That buys a dedicated IPv4, KVM virtualization, 1GB of RAM as the baseline, and a network path chosen for a specific audience. A 1GB KVM box at this level costs about $6.69 a month when annualized, which is still cheap by mainstream standards but three to ten times the floor price.

Three things separate a good yearly deal from a bad one, and none of them is the headline price.

**The billing cycle behind the number.** A lot of listings that look annual are monthly rates multiplied by twelve, and some say so in the fine print: "monthly payments only, cheap to $0.83/month," or "€1/month, €12/year." The 666Clouds catalog is priced monthly in CNY; the yearly figures in this article are my arithmetic, not a discount the provider is advertising. Before you assume you're buying a year, check what cycle the cart actually offers.

**Whether the renewal matches the first payment.** Budget hosts routinely pull people in with a first-year price and then raise it 30–50% at renewal. A $12 first year that renews at $30 isn't a $12 plan. Promotional batches are also finite: when a batch sells out it's gone until the next one, and the next batch usually has different pricing and different stock.

**The virtualization type.** KVM gives the VM its own kernel, so you can run Docker natively, load kernel modules and treat it like a real server. OpenVZ and LXC containers share the host kernel, which caps what you can install and how you can tune it. For most practical purposes KVM is the better buy at the same price.

## Where cheap yearly VPS deals go wrong, and who shouldn't buy

The renewal bump is the first trap, and it's industry-wide: hosts advertise a low first year, then raise the renewal by 30–50% once you're settled in. Before you pay for twelve months, find out what the second year costs and whether the promotional price recurs.

Promotional stock disappears. Budget providers run on volume and thin margins, so the best-priced batches are loss leaders. Popular locations sell out first and stay out until the next cycle. If a plan in the table above is gone when you look, waiting for the next batch is usually better than paying full price for a bigger plan you don't need.

"Unmetered" bandwidth rarely means what people hope. What you're buying is a monthly transfer allowance on a port with a speed cap, and the port is often the real limit. The South Korean plan at 30Mbps and 800GB is a good example: the transfer allowance is generous, the pipe is not.

Disk performance is shared. The 1.2K IOPS in that review is a throttled ceiling, not a hardware limit, and it will show up if you run a database with any real write load.

Support is unmanaged. Ticket-based support at this price level will fix a dead node; it won't configure Nginx for you or debug your PHP stack. If you can't work a Linux command line, budget for the learning curve or pay more elsewhere.

Then there's the ceiling. This catalog stops at 2 vCPU and 2GB of RAM. Nothing here is built for a team, a busy application or anything that needs guaranteed resources.

Don't buy if you need an SLA with credits attached, dedicated CPU rather than shared cores, managed operations, Windows, or IPv6. ## FAQ

### Is annual billing actually cheaper than monthly?

Only when the provider prices it that way. Yearly billing lowers the monthly equivalent at many hosts because they get the cash upfront, but plenty of "yearly" prices are just twelve monthly payments added together. For small servers you'll keep running a long time, yearly fits; if you change specs often or want the option to move, monthly billing fits better. Check the cycle options in the cart before you decide.

### KVM or OpenVZ for a cheap yearly VPS?

KVM, for almost everything. It gives the VM full kernel isolation, so Docker runs natively, custom kernel modules load, and the machine behaves like a real server. OpenVZ and LXC share the host kernel, which limits what you can install and how you can tune it. OpenVZ is acceptable for a pure proxy or a throwaway test box; it's a poor fit for anything you plan to keep.

### Is 1GB of RAM enough?

For one service, yes. A VPN endpoint, a static site, a bot or a monitoring agent all run comfortably in 1GB. The moment you stack a web server, a database and a control panel on the same box, you'll feel it. A WordPress site with caching works, but there's little margin.

### What happens if I exceed the monthly transfer?

The allowance resets each month; going over typically means throttling or suspension until the next cycle, depending on the provider's terms. Since the transfer is metered rather than unlimited, size the plan by what you actually push out. A media-heavy project should start at the 4TB plan rather than discovering the limit mid-month.

### What should I check before paying for a year?

Four things: the renewal price, the refund window, the payment methods accepted (666Clouds takes Alipay, WeChat and PayPal), and whether the plan is billed monthly or annually. Get those four straight and a yearly VPS is a low-risk purchase; skip them and you're gambling on terms you haven't read.
